OUR RESEARCH, EXPLAINED

Understanding how cancer talks to the immune system

Cancer cells do not live alone. They are surrounded by immune cells and other cells that can influence whether a tumour grows, spreads, or responds to treatment. Our lab studies these conversations and looks for ways to make cancer treatments work better.

THE TUMOUR NEIGHBOURHOOD

A tumour is more than a group of cancer cells

Think of a tumour as a neighbourhood. Cancer cells live beside immune cells, blood vessels, and supporting cells. Together, these cells create what scientists call the tumour microenvironment.

Some immune cells normally recognize and remove dangerous cells. But cancer can send signals that confuse these immune cells or change how they behave. When this happens, the tumour may become harder to treat.

THE QUESTIONS WE ASK

Three parts of the puzzle

How do cancer cells quiet the immune system?

We study the messages cancer cells send to immune cells, including macrophages and microglia, and how those messages can weaken an anti-cancer response.

Why do some cancer cells survive treatment?

Tumours contain different kinds of cells. Some may be more resistant than others. We investigate the genes and cell states that help cancer survive therapy.

Can we find a weak point?

By identifying the pathways cancer depends on, we hope to reveal possible targets and biomarkers for future research and treatment strategies.

HOW WE STUDY IT

From gene switches to maps of individual cells

A

Testing genes

We use CRISPR-based tools to switch genes off and learn which ones help cancer grow, avoid immune cells, or resist treatment.

B

Studying cells one at a time

Single-cell technologies help us see how individual cancer and immune cells differ from one another.

C

Mapping where cells live

Imaging and spatial profiling show where cells are located and which cells are communicating inside a tumour.

OUR FOCUS

Glioblastoma and colorectal cancer

Our research focuses particularly on glioblastoma, an aggressive brain cancer, and colorectal cancer. Although these cancers are different, both can use their surrounding environment to shape immune responses and resist treatment.

Understanding these processes may help researchers identify new ways to study treatment resistance and improve future immunotherapy strategies.

PLAIN-LANGUAGE GLOSSARY

A few useful terms

Glioblastoma
An aggressive cancer that begins in the brain.
Macrophage
A type of immune cell that can remove damaged material, respond to danger, and influence other immune cells.
Immunotherapy
A treatment approach designed to help the immune system recognize or attack cancer.
Tumour microenvironment
The cancer cells, immune cells, blood vessels, and other nearby cells and signals that surround a tumour.
